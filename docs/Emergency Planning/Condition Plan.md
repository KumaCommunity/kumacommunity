# Condition Plan

A Condition Plan describes how you will respond to a specific situation. It's a
planning format for this project, not a formal emergency-management standard.

A condition might be:

- An extended power outage
- A severe winter storm
- A wildfire
- A major infrastructure disruption
- A civil disturbance affecting local travel or services

You cannot predict every emergency, but you can decide what to watch for, what
to prepare, and who will do what.

## Purpose

A Condition Plan records:

- The event or condition being addressed
- The trigger for taking action
- The intended response and objective
- Assigned responsibilities
- Related plans and checklists
- When to stand down or reassess

Agree on an initial response in advance, then adjust it to current conditions
and official guidance.

## Step 1: Define the Condition

At the top of the plan, record:

- The condition name
- Severity level, if useful
- Date last reviewed

Example:

**Condition:** Severe Winter Storm  
**Level:** Winter Storm Warning Issued  
**Last reviewed:** [Date]

Be specific. "Bad weather" is difficult to act on. "The National Weather Service
issues a Winter Storm Warning for our area" is a recognizable event. A watch or
advisory may call for earlier preparations, so don't wait for a warning if
conditions are already becoming unsafe.

## Step 2: Define the Trigger

What causes you to begin the plan or move to a different response?

Possible triggers:

- An official weather warning or evacuation instruction
- A utility outage lasting more than a chosen number of hours
- Road closures affecting access to your home
- A public safety advisory
- Conditions at home becoming unsafe, even without an official announcement

Write down who monitors these conditions and where they will get updates. A
trigger should be clear enough to recognize, but it is not a reason to ignore
immediate danger or updated official instructions.

## Step 3: Define the Objective

What are you trying to accomplish while the condition is active?

Examples:

- Maintain safe heat, essential power, and drinking water for 72 hours
- Shelter in place while travel is hazardous
- Prepare to use a specific Exfil Plan if evacuation becomes necessary
- Reduce unnecessary travel and exposure

Keep the objective practical and adjust it if circumstances change.

## Step 4: Outline the Actions

Break the response into actions to take before, during, and after the event.

### Before

What can you do when the condition is forecast or becomes possible?

- Top off vehicle fuel when practical
- Charge backup batteries
- Retrieve supplies from storage
- Stage food, water, medication, and other essentials
- Review official alerts and check on household members

### During

What changes when the condition is active?

- Reduce or stop unnecessary travel
- Use backup power **only with equipment and connections designed for that
  purpose**; operate fuel-burning generators outdoors, well away from doors,
  windows, and vents
- Begin the check-ins specified in your PACE Plan
- Secure the property where safe to do so
- Reassess whether remaining in place is still safe

### After

What needs attention once the immediate danger has passed?

- Check for injuries or medical needs
- Inspect the property when it is safe to do so
- Document damage and prepare insurance information if needed
- Restock consumed supplies
- Check for hazards that may remain after the warning or outage ends

## Step 5: Assign Responsibilities

If more than one person is involved, decide who will handle each task and who
can take over if that person is unavailable.

- Who gathers supplies?
- Who manages communications and check-ins?
- Who handles transportation?
- Who monitors changing conditions and official updates?
- Who looks after children, pets, or anyone needing assistance?

For a one-person household, write down the tasks and identify anyone you may
need to contact for help.

## Step 6: Link Supporting Plans

Reference other documents rather than repeating their details here.

Examples:

- **Exfil Plan:** Where to go and how to evacuate
- **PACE Plan:** How to stay in contact if a communication method fails
- **Go Bag Checklist:** What needs to be packed or staged

Use the current names and locations of those documents in your own copy of the plan.

## Step 7: Define When to Stand Down or Reassess

What tells you the initial response is no longer needed, or that a different
response is required?

- An official all-clear or updated public safety guidance
- Utilities restored and operating safely
- Travel routes reopened and conditions verified
- The immediate threat has passed
- The current shelter or location is no longer safe

An official warning ending does not necessarily mean all hazards are gone.
Record what needs to be checked before resuming normal activities.

## Step 8: Review and Update

After a drill or real event, ask:

- What worked and what did not?
- Were the triggers clear?
- Did anyone lack information or supplies?
- Were the timelines realistic?
- Did conditions require a change to the plan?

Make the necessary changes and update the review date.
