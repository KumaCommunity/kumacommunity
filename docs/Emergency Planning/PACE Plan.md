# PACE Plan

A PACE Plan identifies how a household or group will stay in contact when its
usual communication method is unavailable.

PACE stands for:

- **Primary**
- **Alternate**
- **Contingency**
- **Emergency**

You do not need expensive equipment for every level. You need methods the people
involved can use, and a clear agreement about when to try each one.

## Purpose

A PACE Plan improves the chances of maintaining contact during a disruption. It
does not guarantee communication, especially when multiple methods depend on the
same power, provider, or infrastructure.

For each level, identify the method, who has access to it, and what to do if it
fails.

## Step 1: Primary (P)

The **Primary** method is what you expect to use under normal conditions.

Examples:

- Cell phone call
- Group text thread
- Encrypted messaging app

Document:

- The specific method
- Phone numbers, usernames, or other contact information
- Apps or accounts required
- Expected response time before trying the next method

Give people enough time to miss a message for ordinary reasons without
automatically treating it as an emergency.

## Step 2: Alternate (A)

The **Alternate** method is your next option when the primary method does not work.

Examples:

- SMS if voice calls fail
- A different messaging platform
- Email
- A secondary cellular provider
- Wi-Fi calling

Document:

- Which method to try
- Required contact information or accounts
- The conditions that trigger the switch

**Check for shared failure points.** Two messaging apps on the same phone may
both fail if that phone loses power. Voice, SMS, and Wi-Fi calling can also
depend on overlapping networks or services. An alternate method can still be
useful, but know what it does and does not protect against.

## Step 3: Contingency (C)

The **Contingency** method is less convenient but still workable when the usual
options are unavailable.

Examples:

- Scheduled in-person check-ins at a known location
- Radio communication (FRS, GMRS, or amateur radio)
- Meshtastic or MeshCore devices
- A satellite messaging service that supports ordinary messages

These options may need equipment, compatible settings, training, or a service subscription.

Document:

- Radio channel or frequency and any required settings
- Check-in times and locations
- Which devices each participant has
- Battery and charging requirements
- Any licensing requirements that apply

In the United States, compliant FRS use does not require an individual license,
while GMRS and amateur radio generally do. Meshtastic and MeshCore are separate
systems and should not be assumed to communicate with each other. Test the exact
equipment and network configuration you intend to use.

## Step 4: Emergency (E)

The **Emergency** level is for urgent circumstances when other ways of
coordinating are unavailable or unsuitable.

Possible arrangements include:

- A prearranged meeting location and time
- A trusted person who can carry information when it is safe to do so
- Contacting emergency services by any available means
- A distress device appropriate to the situation

If your plan includes a **406 MHz personal locator beacon (PLB)**, understand
its purpose: it sends a distress alert to search-and-rescue authorities. It is
**not** a routine family messaging or check-in device. Do not activate it for
practice or a missed check-in; use the manufacturer's self-test procedure only.

Document:

- What counts as an emergency requiring this level
- Where participants should go, if travel is safe
- How long to wait and what to do next
- How to request help when ordinary communications are unavailable

Avoid improvised signals that could create a hazard or be misinterpreted.

## Define When to Fail Over

For every level, agree on:

- How long to attempt contact
- What counts as a failed attempt
- Whether to retry before moving to the next method
- What to do if someone is simply unavailable
- When a missed check-in warrants contacting a designated person or emergency services

For example, your plan might call for another message after a missed scheduled
check-in, followed by an alternate channel after a defined interval. Choose
intervals that fit the situation; don't assume every missed message means
someone is in danger.

## Power and Access Considerations

Common failure points include:

- Dead batteries or missing chargers
- Loss of cellular service, internet access, or local power
- Forgotten passwords or inaccessible accounts
- Devices with incompatible radio settings
- People who have not practiced using the backup method

Document:

- Which chargers, cables, and backup batteries are needed
- Where spare equipment is stored
- How authorized users can access necessary account information securely
- How long the equipment can operate without mains power

## Synchronization

Everyone using the plan should know:

- The time zone and check-in schedule
- Which method to use at each level
- The contact information and meeting locations
- What a missed check-in means and what to do next

Use plain language unless the group has a specific, well-understood reason to
use agreed terminology. Avoid creating codes that participants may forget or
misunderstand.

## Keep It Realistic

Choose methods that:

- Are available to the people involved
- Work in the places where they will be used
- Can be powered and maintained
- Are legal to operate and compatible with each other
- Have been tested in realistic conditions

A method that no one has configured or practiced is not a dependable backup.

## Review and Practice

Periodically check:

- Backup contacts and account access
- Battery life and charging equipment
- Radio range and device compatibility, if applicable
- Travel time to meeting locations
- Whether participants still know the check-in schedule

Update the plan after a test, a change in equipment, or a real disruption.
